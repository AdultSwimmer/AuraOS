No, inflection and indicative are not synonyms. They represent entirely different linguistic concepts, even though both relate to grammar. [1, 2, 3, 4]
Here is the difference between the two terms at a glance:
Inflection [1]
Inflection refers to the way words change form to express different grammatical categories like tense, case, voice, or number. It is the physical modification of a word. [1, 2, 3]
* Example: Adding "-ed" to "walk" to make it "walked," or changing "mouse" to "mice". [1]
Indicative [1]
The indicative (or indicative mood) is a specific grammatical category for verbs used to state a fact, declare something, or ask a question. It is a type of mood, not the physical change to the word itself. [1, 2]
* Example: Saying "The dog barks" is in the indicative mood.
How They Intersect (The One Connection)
An inflection can sometimes be used to create the indicative mood, but they are not interchangeable. For instance, adding an "s" to the end of a verb in the third-person singular (e.g., "he runs") is an inflection that places the verb into the present indicative. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
How to Use Them Correctly
* Use inflection when referring to word endings, modifications, or changes in vocal pitch/tone.
* Use indicative (often followed by the word of) when saying something acts as a sign, clue, or factual statement. (e.g., "His flushed face was indicative of a fever.") [1, 2, 3, 4]
